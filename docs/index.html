The evidence from 2008 to 2023

National's share of special votes has been below its share of ordinary votes in every election since 2008, and the Greens' has always been above. In Labour-v-National seats, specials moved the margin towards Labour every time, but the size of that swing varied more than fourfold between elections.

Methodology

  1. Starting point. The Electoral Commission's preliminary count for every party and electorate, plus its estimate of the special votes still to be counted.
  2. Party vote on specials. For 2014, 2017, 2020 and 2023, each party's share of special votes was compared with its share of ordinary votes, separately for domestic and overseas specials, from the Commission's voting-place statistics. Each run draws one of those years as a template and applies its ratios to the 2026 preliminary shares. The domestic lean is set below its historical level because election-day enrolment has ended, but only modestly, given how much late enrolment moved earlier.
  3. Valid specials. A lower share of specials is assumed to count than in past elections, because the Commission expected many more to be thrown out from people who weren't enrolled.
  4. Electorates. Each seat's special-vote total comes from its 2023 ratio of special to ordinary votes (or its predecessor seat's), scaled to the 2026 total. In Labour-v-National seats, the swing on specials is the template year's average swing plus that seat's own historical lean, shrunk towards the average, plus noise. The Māori seats use the template year's swing between Te Pāti Māori and Labour.
  5. Candidates. Every seat is modelled with the candidates actually standing in 2026, taken from the Commission's electorate pages. The Commission's 2023 split-vote statistics show how each party's voters spread their electorate vote across the candidates on offer. From these the model estimates where a party's voters go when it has no candidate. In each seat these flows are calibrated to reproduce the 2026 ordinary-vote candidate results exactly, then applied to the seat's special party vote. The difference between that and a field with every major party standing is added to the seat's swing. — seats lack a candidate from a party with 3% or more of their party vote. The effect is largest in Hamilton East, which has no Green or ACT candidate: the missing Green candidate outweighs the missing ACT one, adding — to Labour's special-vote margin. That lifts Labour's chance of taking the seat from — to —.
  6. Seats. 120 seats go by Sainte-Laguë to parties with 5% of the party vote or an electorate. Electorate wins beyond a party's entitlement are kept as overhang, and the House grows to fit.
  7. One draw per run. The party vote and every electorate race in a run come from the same draw of special votes, so the events combine as they would on the night.

  • Run on the preliminary votes with no specials, the seat allocation reproduces the Commission's projection of — seats exactly, party by party.

  • Using 2008-2023 instead of 2017-2023 for each seat's own lean barely changes the result (see the scenarios table).
  • Backtest on — Labour-v-National seat results from 2017-2023, testing each year using only the other years' data. The swing method misses the special-vote margin by — (root-mean-square), and by — with the candidate-field adjustment. In the — results where a major party had no candidate, the figures are — and —. That improvement is within noise: in past elections a missing candidate moved the special-vote swing too little to detect against ordinary seat-to-seat variation. The adjustment rests mainly on the split-vote evidence.
  • Flows alone, without the swing method, did worse at — (or — given each seat's actual special party vote). Special voters split their electorate vote somewhat differently from ordinary voters of the same party, so the flows are used only for the effect of the candidate field.

Not modelled

  • The age and enrolment-status mix of this year's specials. The Commission publishes this in its detailed statistics after the official count.
  • Corrections from the official recount of ordinary votes. These usually move margins by tens of votes, which would only matter in Wairarapa and Wellington Bays.
  • Judicial recounts, legal challenges and coalition negotiations. The scenarios describe what the numbers allow.
  • New 2025 electorate boundaries. Seats are matched to their closest predecessor.

Glossary

Terms specific to this analysis.

Central case
The main settings behind the headline numbers. Variants change one assumption at a time.
Run
One simulated count of all the special votes. The central case has 20,000.
Template year
The past election whose special-vote pattern a run applies (2014, 2017, 2020 or 2023).
Lean
How much more left-leaning this year's specials are than ordinary votes, as a share of the template year's (85% in the central case).
Valid share
The share of special votes expected to count (85% in the central case).
Seat lean
A seat's own historical tendency on specials compared with the national average.
Swing on specials
How far a two-candidate margin moves once specials are added, as a share of the special votes.
Candidate field
The candidates actually standing in a seat. Where a party has no candidate, its voters' electorate votes go where the 2023 split-vote data shows they go.

National, ACT and NZ First.
